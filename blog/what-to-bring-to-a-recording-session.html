What to Bring to a Recording Session: A Complete Checklist for Artists

By AMPED UP AUDIO · November 8, 2026

Most wasted studio time is not about the recording itself — it is about a file that will not open, lyrics that live in a screenshot, or a beat that only exists as a link nobody can download in the room. Bringing the right things makes the session start when it is supposed to start. At AMPED UP AUDIO in El Monte, recording is $40/hour · with engineer included — this checklist covers what to bring so that time goes into takes, not searching for files.

AMPED UP AUDIO has been operating since October 2025. You do not need to bring recording equipment — that side is handled in the room. What you bring is the material, the plan, and the basics that keep you comfortable and focused while you perform.

1. Your music files, in the best quality you have

Recording: $40/hour · with engineer

Every recording session at AMPED is $40/hour with engineer included. Mixing stays flat and separate: $100 for a 2-track mix, $150 for a full stem mix.

  • Your beat or instrumental, ready to play. Bring the actual file, not just a streaming link. A link can be region-locked, removed, or impossible to download cleanly once you are already on the clock.
  • The highest-quality version you have. If you have a lossless version as well as a compressed one, bring the lossless one. A cleaner starting file gives the recording and the mix more to work with.
  • Stems as well, if you have them. If your beat came with separate stems, bring those too — even if you plan to record over the single beat file. Having stems on hand keeps your mixing options open later.
  • A backup copy. Keep the same files in a second place — a phone, a drive, or a cloud folder you can open quickly. One corrupted file should never cost a session.

Which files you bring also decides which mix fits later. For the breakdown, see 2-Track Mix vs. Full Stem Mix, and for what a booked session actually covers, see Recording in El Monte: What a Session Includes.

2. Your lyrics, in a form you can actually read

  • Lyrics you can read at a glance. Bring them typed or clearly written, in an order that matches how the song runs. Reading from a blurry photo of a notebook page slows every take down.
  • The current version, not an old draft. Check before you leave that the version you bring is the version you actually want to record — changed hooks and swapped verses are easy to miss at home and expensive to sort out mid-session.
  • Notes on parts and structure. Mark where doubles, ad-libs, harmonies, or a second voice come in, if you plan them. A short note next to the part is enough — it keeps the plan clear without stopping the session to explain it.
  • Your material learned, not just written. Bringing the lyrics is not the same as knowing them. The more of the performance you have down before you arrive, the more of the session goes into getting a great take instead of a first rehearsal. See How to Prepare for Your First Recording Session.

3. A reference track, if you have a sound in mind

A reference is simply an existing song that points at the feeling, energy, or vocal approach you want. It is not about copying it — it is the fastest way to explain a direction in words that can otherwise take a long time to land.

  • One or two references is enough. Pick the songs that genuinely sound closest to what you are making, and be ready to say what it is about them — the vocal closeness, the energy, the space around the voice.
  • Bring the song, not just the title. Have it saved or easy to pull up, so the room can listen to the same version you mean.
  • No reference is fine too. If you do not have one, say so plainly. The engineer can work from your performance and your description — a forced reference helps less than an honest one. For more on that working relationship, see What a Recording Engineer Actually Does.

4. The basics that keep you performing well

Recording is physical work. Your voice and your energy are the instrument being recorded, so the small comforts are not extras — they protect the take.

  • Water. Bring water and drink it through the session. A dry, tired voice shows up on the recording, and short breaks to drink are cheaper than re-recording a worn-out section.
  • Something light to eat beforehand. Arrive fed enough to focus, rather than trying to perform through being hungry and distracted.
  • A phone charger. If your lyrics, files, or notes live on your phone, a dead battery in the middle of a session is a real problem with a very simple fix.
  • Comfortable clothes and a clear head. You will stand, repeat lines, and listen closely for a while. Arrive rested where you can, and give the performance your full attention — see Vocal Recording Tips: How to Get a Better Take for how delivery carries a take.

5. A simple plan for the time you booked

  • Know your priority song. If you are working on more than one song, decide in advance which one matters most and start there, while your voice and focus are freshest.
  • Be realistic about how much fits. One song finished well is worth more than several songs rushed and left unfinished. For honest timing, see How Long Does a Recording Session Take?
  • Keep your files labeled as you go. Note which take was the keeper and what each part is while it is still fresh. Messy, unlabeled files cost time in mixing — one of the points in Common Home-Recording Mistakes.
  • Know what happens after recording. Recording and mixing are separate steps. Mixing afterwards is flat: a 2-track mix is $100 and a full stem mix is $150. Planning for that from the start avoids surprises at the end.

The short version

Bring the beat as a real file in the best quality you have — plus stems and a backup if you have them — your current lyrics in a readable form, a reference track if you have a sound in mind, water, and a charger. Arrive knowing the material and knowing which song comes first.
